# The digital trace

The **digital trace** is the portion of cultural activity that has been recorded in machine-readable form. In HLC it mediates between the broad [[foundations/cultural-hologram|cultural hologram]] and the material a model might encounter during training. Websites, digitized books, captions, photographs, and transcripts are possible elements of that trace; their presence does not imply that any particular model trained on them.

Recording changes what can travel. A conversation becomes searchable after transcription, but tone, gesture, silence, and context may be reduced or lost. A photograph preserves visible detail while leaving other sensory and social information outside its frame. Different recording systems make different aspects of an event legible.

Selection happens repeatedly. Someone must have the means and reason to produce a record; the record must survive; it must be digitized or born digital; and it must become available to a particular collection. HLC uses this chain to ask why some subjects appear abundantly represented while others appear through a narrow set of descriptions. These questions connect directly to [[critique/ideology-and-power|ideology and power]].

The digital trace is also distinct from a training corpus. A developer’s collection, filtering, licensing, and preprocessing decisions form another boundary, described here as the [[foundations/corpus-membrane|corpus membrane]]. Treating the entire internet as if it were the model’s literal memory erases this distinction.

For practice, ask what sort of record would support an answer. A description of an undocumented technique requires a different evidentiary route from a summary of a supplied manual. This simple question often reveals when a fluent account is outrunning the trace available to it.

## Three records of one practice

Imagine trying to understand how a community kitchen works. Its public website lists hours and services. A volunteer manual explains procedures. A participant’s account describes whether newcomers feel welcome. Each is a digital record, yet each answers a different question. Reading all three expands the account without guaranteeing completeness.

Now imagine that only the website is supplied to a model. The answer may still discuss training, trust, conflict, and informal care because those topics commonly accompany community institutions. Some suggestions could be sensible, but they should be presented as possibilities to investigate. They are not observations of this kitchen. The missing manual and participant account remain missing even if the model produces paragraphs that resemble them.

This example separates an inferred pattern from a sourced description. HLC becomes more useful when that separation survives the excitement of a richly connected answer.
